# Field-Effect Transistors (FETs)

## 1. Fundamentals and Device Types

**Core Concept**

A field-effect transistor (**FET**) is a voltage-controlled current source.

This page covers metal-oxide-semiconductor FETs (**MOSFETs**) and junction FETs (**JFETs**).

In the ideal model, the **gate** draws zero direct current (**DC**). As a result, the input impedance is infinite.

A real gate has small leakage current and capacitance. Current flows briefly when the gate charges or discharges during switching.

**Key Definitions**

* **Gate-source voltage, V<sub>GS</sub>:** This is the control voltage. It changes the channel conduction. You can think of it as a valve control.
* **Drain-source voltage, V<sub>DS</sub>:** This voltage drives current through the channel. You can think of it as pressure across the channel.
* **Threshold voltage, V<sub>th</sub>:** This is the specified <i>V<sub>GS</sub></i> where an enhancement channel starts to form.
* **Zero-gate drain current, I<sub>DSS</sub>:** This is the specified JFET or depletion-device current at <i>V<sub>GS</sub> = 0</i>.

The original notes describe <i>I<sub>DSS</sub></i> as the maximum zero-bias current. A depletion MOSFET can conduct more current when positive gate drive is permitted.

**Core Equations**

For a simplified square-law MOSFET in saturation:

* <i>I<sub>D</sub> = k &times; (V<sub>GS</sub> - V<sub>th</sub>)<sup>2</sup></i>

The original rough current-to-voltage ratio is <i>I<sub>D</sub> / V<sub>GS</sub></i>. The small-signal definition of **transconductance** is:

* <i>g<sub>m</sub> = dI<sub>D</sub> / dV<sub>GS</sub></i>

For the simplified square-law model:

* <i>g<sub>m</sub> = 2I<sub>D</sub> / (V<sub>GS</sub> - V<sub>th</sub>)</i>

For an unloaded common-source stage:

* <i>A<sub>v</sub> &asymp; -g<sub>m</sub> &times; R<sub>D</sub></i>

**Enhancement and Depletion Devices**

**Enhancement Mode (Normally Off):**

* If <i>V<sub>GS</sub> &lt; V<sub>th</sub></i>, no strong channel exists. In the ideal model, <i>I<sub>DS</sub> = 0</i>.
* Apply gate bias to turn the device on.
* Enhancement MOSFETs are standard devices for logic and power applications.

**Depletion Mode (Normally On):**

* A channel exists at <i>V<sub>GS</sub> = 0</i>.
* For an N-channel device, negative gate bias decreases the current and can stop it.
* The specified zero-bias current is <i>I<sub>DSS</sub></i>.

## 2. MOSFET Operation and Terminal Roles

<MosfetCalc />

### N-Channel Operating Regions

The following boundaries use the ideal long-channel model.

1. **Cutoff (Open Switch)**

   * **Condition:** <i>V<sub>GS</sub> &lt; V<sub>th</sub></i>.
   * **Result:** <i>I<sub>DS</sub> &asymp; 0</i>. The switch is **off**.
   * A real device can have a small leakage current.

2. **Ohmic or Linear Region (Resistor)**

   * **Condition:** <i>V<sub>GS</sub> &gt; V<sub>th</sub></i> and <i>V<sub>DS</sub> &lt; V<sub>GS</sub> - V<sub>th</sub></i>.
   * **Behavior:** The device operates as a voltage-controlled resistor.
   * For small <i>V<sub>DS</sub></i>, drain current increases approximately linearly with <i>V<sub>DS</sub></i>. The basic relationship is <i>I = V/R</i>.
   * **Uses:** Variable resistance and low-loss power switching.

3. **Active or Saturation Region (Current Source)**

   * **Condition:** <i>V<sub>DS</sub> &ge; V<sub>GS</sub> - V<sub>th</sub></i>.
   * **Behavior:** The device operates approximately as a constant-current source.
   * The channel is pinched off near the drain.
   * In the ideal model, a further increase in <i>V<sub>DS</sub></i> does not increase current.
   * Gate drive, <i>V<sub>GS</sub></i>, primarily sets the current.
   * A real device has some current change because of channel-length modulation.

### Source and Drain Design Intuition

Some small-signal FET structures are approximately symmetrical. In those devices, terminal voltage can determine the effective source and drain.

* **N-channel source:** The lower-voltage channel terminal operates as the source.
* **P-channel source:** The higher-voltage channel terminal operates as the source.

Power MOSFETs are not fully symmetrical. Their body connection and intrinsic body diode give the terminals a fixed practical orientation.

### Negative-Drain Trap

Consider an N-channel MOSFET that switches a signal line to ground.

1. You assume that the drain is the high-side terminal.
2. You set the gate to 0 V to turn the MOSFET off.
3. The signal line then decreases to -2 V.
4. That terminal is now below the grounded terminal.
5. It becomes the effective source in a symmetrical small-signal device.
6. The gate-source voltage becomes:

   <i>V<sub>GS</sub> = V<sub>G</sub> - V<sub>true source</sub> = 0 V - (-2 V) = +2 V</i>

7. The FET can turn on unintentionally.

To keep the device off, make the gate more negative than the most-negative signal. Also check the body-diode path in a practical MOSFET.

## 3. JFET and Depletion-Mode Operation

Junction field-effect transistors (**JFETs**) are **normally on**. Their zero-bias behavior is the reverse of an enhancement MOSFET.

**How an N-Channel JFET Operates**

* At 0 V gate bias, current is approximately <i>I<sub>DSS</sub></i>.
* A more-negative gate voltage narrows the channel and eventually pinches it off.
* The gate is a diode junction to the channel.
* **Do not forward bias the gate.** For an N-channel JFET, do not make the gate positive relative to the source.
* Forward bias causes gate current and can damage the device.

**JFET Compared with a Depletion MOSFET**

* **Similarity:** Both devices are normally on. Negative <i>V<sub>GS</sub></i> decreases N-channel current.
* **Difference:** A depletion MOSFET has an insulated gate.
* You can drive a depletion-MOSFET gate positive to get more current without forward-biasing a gate junction.
* JFETs are useful for **low-noise** linear circuits, audio front ends, and amplifiers.
* JFETs have no thin gate-oxide layer. As a result, they are generally less susceptible to electrostatic-discharge damage than MOSFETs.

## 4. Basic Applications

### High-Impedance Buffers and Source Followers

Use a FET when bipolar-junction-transistor (**BJT**) input current would load the signal source.

The approximate source-follower gain is:

* <i>A<sub>v</sub> = 1 / [1 + 1 / (g<sub>m</sub> &times; R<sub>L</sub>)]</i>

The approximate output impedance is <i>1/g<sub>m</sub></i>.

Because <i>g<sub>m</sub></i> changes with signal current, output impedance also changes. The original notes call this change a "wobble." It can cause distortion.

Use a constant-current-source load or a negative supply to stabilize the operating point.

### Analog Switches

FETs in the **ohmic region** operate as low-resistance switches. Applications include multiplexers and [sample-and-hold](<../../03-Signal-Modulation/Data-convertes/Sample-holding.md#1-basic-circuit>) circuits.

### Variable Resistors

In the ohmic region, a change in <i>V<sub>GS</sub></i> changes <i>R<sub>DS</sub></i>.

You can use this effect for voltage-controlled volume. Device range and distortion limit this application.

### Power Switching

MOSFETs dominate many power-switching applications because <i>R<sub>DS(on)</sub></i> can be low.

Their resistive on-state and positive temperature coefficient make parallel operation comparatively easy.

### Feedthrough

An ideal off switch is an air gap. A real MOSFET has **drain-source capacitance, C<sub>ds</sub>**.

* **Effect:** High-frequency signals can cross this capacitance when the switch is off. The unwanted signal then appears at the output.
* **Solution:** Use a T-switch or single-pole, double-throw (**SPDT**) arrangement. Ground the signal path when the switch is off.

### Floating Output and Ghost Signals

A floating, high-impedance output can receive noise and leakage signals.

* **Resistor solution:** Add a load resistor from the output to ground. A typical starting range is 1 k&Omega; to 100 k&Omega;.
* **Low-value example:** A 1 k&Omega; resistor decreases feedthrough. It also forms a divider with <i>R<sub>on</sub></i> and causes signal loss.
* **High-value example:** A 1 M&Omega; resistor causes little signal loss. It can be too weak to discharge capacitive leakage.
* **Preferred solution:** Use a second switch or an SPDT switch. Actively ground the output when the signal is disconnected.

### CMOS Transmission Gate

A complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (**CMOS**) transmission gate uses two device types.

**Problem:**

1. An N-channel MOSFET passes a low voltage well.
2. As its source voltage increases, <i>V<sub>GS</sub></i> decreases.
3. The device then passes a high voltage poorly and can turn off.
4. A P-channel MOSFET has the complementary behavior.

**Solution:**

* Connect an N-channel MOSFET and a P-channel MOSFET in parallel.
* One device handles low voltages, and the other handles high voltages.
* The pair permits **rail-to-rail** signal switching.

### Distortion and Charge Injection

* **Total harmonic distortion (THD):** <i>R<sub>on</sub></i> changes as signal voltage changes. The effective <i>V<sub>GS</sub></i> also changes. This nonlinear resistance causes distortion.
* **[Charge injection](<../../03-Signal-Modulation/Data-convertes/Sample-holding.md#charge-injection-and-pedestal-error>):** Gate capacitance stores charge. When the switch turns off, some charge can enter the signal path and cause a voltage spike.
* **Correction:** Use an analog-switch integrated circuit (**IC**) that specifies low charge injection.

## 6. Current Sinks and Sources

A **current sink** draws a specified current. A **current source** supplies a specified current.

An ideal circuit keeps this current constant for all node voltages. A real circuit does this only within its compliance-voltage range.

### Why Use a Current Sink?

Assume that an [emitter follower](<./02-BJTs.md#5-amplifier-configurations>) must supply a fixed current, <i>X</i>.

* **Resistor method:** Connect a resistor to the negative rail. This method uses more power and decreases input impedance.
* **Active-sink method:** Use a JFET or BJT. An active device maintains higher impedance and better current control.

### JFET Current-Sink Example

A JFET is a normally-on depletion device. As a result, you can use source resistance for self-bias.

    NPN emitter follower with a JFET current sink. Image source: <i>The Art of Electronics</i>, Figure 3.24.
  </figcaption>

**Self-Bias Sequence:**

1. Tie the gate to -12 V.
2. The source voltage is <i>-12 V + I<sub>D</sub> &times; R<sub>S</sub></i>.
3. The result is:

   <i>V<sub>GS</sub> = V<sub>G</sub> - V<sub>S</sub> = -12 V - (-12 V + I<sub>D</sub>R<sub>S</sub>) = -I<sub>D</sub>R<sub>S</sub></i>

4. Select <i>R<sub>S</sub></i> to produce the necessary negative <i>V<sub>GS</sub></i>.
5. For a 5 mA design:

   <i>R<sub>S</sub> = |V<sub>GS(required)</sub>| / 5 mA</i>

**JFET-Sink Limitation:**

<i>I<sub>DSS</sub></i> can change greatly between parts. As a result, JFET current can be difficult to predict.

A JFET sink is usually not consistent enough for a tight tolerance. Use a BJT sink when the application needs better predictability.

**Open-Loop Analysis Without R<sub>f</sub>:**

* Bias the JFET. An alternating-current (**AC**) drain current, <i>I<sub>D</sub></i>, then flows.
* The [op-amp](<../../03-Signal-Modulation/Amplifiers/01-op-amps.md#1-op-amp-fundamentals>) operates as a current-to-voltage converter.
* The original notes write <i>V<sub>out</sub> = g<sub>m</sub> &times; R<sub>gain</sub></i>. Dimensionally, this product is the voltage-gain magnitude.
* The voltage relationship is:

  <i>V<sub>out</sub> = V<sub>in</sub> &times; g<sub>m</sub> &times; R<sub>gain</sub></i>

**Numerical Example:**

* <i>g<sub>m</sub> = 25 mS</i>.
* <i>R = 100 k&Omega;</i>.
* Gain is 2500.
* A 1 mV input produces a 2.5 V output.

**Closed-Loop [Negative Feedback](<../../03-Signal-Modulation/Amplifiers/01-op-amps.md#negative-feedback>):**

1. Input voltage increases.
2. <i>V<sub>GS</sub></i> moves closer to 0 V.
3. The JFET conducts more current.
4. The op-amp output increases.
5. Current into the JFET source increases.
6. Source voltage increases.
7. Gate and source voltages then increase together.
8. As a result, <i>V<sub>GS</sub></i> remains almost constant.

The feedback-resistor ratio, <i>R<sub>f</sub> / R<sub>g</sub></i>, sets the closed-loop gain. Transistor parameters no longer set it directly.

## 8. Gate-Drive Dynamics

Gate-drive behavior is important in switching circuits, motor drivers, and power supplies.

### Static Gate Current

* **Ideal:** The gate is isolated by an oxide or another insulator. The "glass" analogy describes this insulation. In the ideal model, <i>I<sub>G</sub> = 0</i>.
* **Real:** Small leakage current exists. It can be in the picoampere range.
* **Protection devices:** Some discrete MOSFETs include Zener or other electrostatic-discharge protection. These devices can leak nanoamperes or microamperes.
* **Design rule:** Check the datasheet before you use a protected MOSFET in an ultra-high-impedance electrometer circuit.

### Dynamic Gate Current

Use a **bucket or capacitor analogy** for the gate.

* **DC:** After the gate capacitance charges, drive current stops except for leakage.
* **Turn-on:** The driver must put charge into the gate.
* **Turn-off:** The driver must remove charge from the gate.
* **Average switching-current relationship:** <i>I = Q<sub>gate</sub> / time</i>.

**Example:**

* [Gate charge](<../../02-Power/Power%20Control/Gate-Drivers.md#1-gate-charge-and-switching>) is 50 nC.
* Necessary switching time is 100 ns.
* Required current is <i>50 nC / 100 ns = 0.5 A</i>.

Use a dedicated **gate-driver IC** to supply these short high-current pulses.

A standard 20 mA general-purpose input/output (**[GPIO](<../../04-Digital-Interfaces/Embedded-Systems.md#pins-and-external-circuits>)**) pin is too weak for fast switching in this example.

### Miller Plateau

During gate charging, gate voltage first increases, then becomes almost constant, and then increases again.

1. **Region 1: Charge C<sub>GS</sub>**

   * Gate voltage increases approximately linearly.
   * The MOSFET is off.

2. **Region 2: Miller Plateau**

   * The MOSFET turns on, and drain voltage decreases.
   * The changing drain voltage moves charge through gate-drain capacitance, <i>C<sub>gd</sub></i>.
   * Driver current continues, but gate voltage stays almost constant.
   * The feedback capacitance uses the supplied charge during this interval.

3. **Region 3: Complete the Gate Charge**

   * The drain transition is complete.
   * The device moves toward the fully enhanced ohmic on-state.
   * Gate voltage increases to the driver level.

During the plateau, the MOSFET is partially on and operates as a resistor. It dissipates heat.

Supply sufficient gate current to move through the plateau quickly.

## 9. Power MOSFET Design

This section gives requirements for high-current devices.

### Thermal Stability of R<sub>DS(on)</sub>

* <i>R<sub>DS(on)</sub></i> increases with temperature. This is a positive temperature coefficient.
* In the fully enhanced switching state, a hot die area develops more resistance.
* Current then moves toward cooler die areas.
* This behavior gives a sharing advantage relative to BJTs and makes parallel MOSFET operation easier.
* Calculate power dissipation with the worst-case resistance.
* At 125&deg;C, resistance can be 1.5 times its specified 25&deg;C value.

This sharing benefit does not remove the linear-mode hot-spot risk described later.

### Body Diode

* A power MOSFET has an intrinsic P-N body diode between its source and drain.
* In a suitable circuit orientation, this diode can carry [inductive flyback](<../01-Passives/03-Inductors.md#interactive-the-inductive-kick>) current.
* This path can prevent damaging drain-source overvoltage.
* The body diode can have slow reverse recovery.
* Slow diode turn-off can cause [shoot-through](<../../02-Power/Power%20Control/Gate-Drivers.md#4-high-side-drive-and-dead-time>) current in an [H-bridge](<../../02-Power/Power%20Control/Motor-Drives.md#drive-paths-and-braking>).
* Select a device with suitable fast-recovery behavior when the application requires it.

### Safe Operating Area

The safe-operating-area (**SOA**) graph shows safe combinations of <i>V<sub>DS</sub></i> and <i>I<sub>D</sub></i>.

Designers sometimes call this graph the datasheet "map of death."

**Linear-mode warning:** A switching MOSFET can be a poor variable resistor. Local die hot spots can cause failure inside the calculated total-power limit.

### Ways to Damage a MOSFET

1. **Resistive Heating from Insufficient Gate Voltage**

   * **Cause:** Apply 3.3 V to a gate that requires 10 V for low <i>R<sub>DS(on)</sub></i>.
   * **Result:** The FET does not turn fully on.
   * <i>R<sub>DS</sub></i> stays high, and <i>I<sup>2</sup>R</i> heating can destroy the device.

2. **Inductive Overvoltage**

   * **Cause:** Turn off a motor or [solenoid](<../../02-Power/Power%20Control/Motor-Drives.md#electrical-and-mechanical-behavior>) quickly without a [snubber](<./01-Diodes.md#c-rc-snubbers-and-rcd-clamps>) or clamp.
   * **Result:** Inductor current forces voltage to increase until a clamp, breakdown path, or parasitic path conducts.
   * Excess voltage can puncture device insulation and destroy the MOSFET.

### High-Side Floating Gate

**Scenario:** A ground-referenced logic signal must control a high-side N-channel MOSFET.

**Technique:** Use a discrete current source that pushes current against a gate-source resistor.

This circuit lets the gate-control voltage move upward with the source voltage.

An integrated **high-side driver** performs essentially this function internally.

## 10. Real-World Limitations and Selection

### FET "Softness" Compared with BJT "Stiffness"

At a specified current, BJTs usually have higher <i>g<sub>m</sub></i> than FETs. As a result, a BJT follower usually has lower output impedance.

The original notes call the BJT output "stiff" and the FET output "soft."

* Approximate follower output impedance is <i>1/g<sub>m</sub></i>.
* A FET follower usually has higher output impedance than a BJT follower at the same current.
* As a result, its output voltage changes more when load current changes.
* At extremely low currents, the difference becomes smaller.

### Precision Current Setting

* MOSFET <i>V<sub>GS(th)</sub></i> can differ by as much as 3 V between devices in the same batch.
* As a result, a fixed gate voltage cannot set an accurate drain current.
* BJT <i>V<sub>BE</sub></i> at a specified current is more predictable, but approximately 0.7 V is not an exact constant.
* BJTs usually give better results in high-precision discrete current mirrors and sources.

### Miller Effect and Bandwidth

* A high [source impedance](<../../00-Foundations/00-Foundations.md#the-ideal-source-and-the-real-source>) decreases bandwidth when it interacts with Miller capacitance.
* The capacitance and source impedance make a low-pass network.
* Higher source impedance decreases the cutoff frequency.
* Use a **cascode** to isolate the input device from large output-voltage changes.

### Cascode

* Put a common-gate FET above a common-source FET.
* The upper device keeps the lower-device drain voltage almost constant.
* This arrangement greatly decreases the Miller effect.
* It also greatly increases output impedance.

## 11. Linear Examples and the Danger Zone

The original Art of Electronics note gives this guidance: discrete MOSFETs can make linear amplifiers, but they are frequently not the best choice.

### Discrete-MOSFET Variability and Noise

A device such as the 2N7000 is less predictable than a BJT or JFET in a precision linear circuit.

* **Threshold variation:** <i>V<sub>th</sub></i> can range from 0.8 V to 3 V in one batch.
* A precise DC bias point is not possible without feedback.
* **Noise:** Generic MOSFETs can have higher low-frequency <i>1/f</i> noise than JFETs in audio applications.
* Popcorn noise is a separate burst-noise mechanism. Do not use it as another name for <i>1/f</i> noise.
* Use a JFET or BJT for a low-noise microphone preamplifier. Do not select a generic MOSFET without a noise evaluation.

### CMOS Inverter Linear-Amplifier Method

This is an unconventional low-cost method. The original notes call it a "MacGyver" method.

* A CD4069 or 74HCU04 digital inverter contains a P-channel FET above an N-channel FET.
* Connect a high-value resistor, such as 1 M&Omega;, from output to input.
* This resistor makes the inverter self-bias at <i>V<sub>DD</sub> / 2</i>.
* The inverter then operates as a high-gain linear amplifier.
* **Warning:** Use an **unbuffered** U-series device.
* A buffered inverter has too much gain and can oscillate.

### Linear-Power Hot Spots

This problem occurs when a MOSFET operates as a variable resistor in a linear regulator or Class A amplifier.

* Modern switching MOSFETs are optimized for high cell density.
* With high voltage and high current at the same time, current can concentrate in a small die area.
* This concentration causes localized thermal runaway.
* The MOSFET can fail below its total power rating.
* Use a linear-rated MOSFET, frequently made with older planar technology.
* Also check the **DC line** on the SOA graph.


## Geometry and the body connection

The **width-to-length ratio (W/L)** describes channel geometry in a simple MOSFET model. Greater width can increase current capability and gate capacitance.

A shorter channel can increase current for the same bias in a long-channel approximation. Short-channel effects limit direct use of that approximation in modern devices.

Discrete power MOSFETs usually connect the body to the source internally. This controls body potential and helps suppress unwanted parasitic transistor operation.

Integrated MOSFET bodies can use a shared well connection. Their body voltage can change threshold through the body effect. Do not assume every body is separately accessible.

Gate oxide is thin and tolerates limited electric field. Check gate-to-source limits even when drain voltage remains within its rating.

## LTspice example: JFET self bias

This circuit extends [JFET operation](#3-jfet-and-depletion-mode-operation) with a supply sweep. The gate resistor connects the gate to ground.

Drain current creates a voltage across the source resistor. The source becomes positive relative to the gate, so the gate to source voltage becomes negative.

[Open this circuit beside its topic](</docs/Discrete-Components/Semicondctors/MOSFETs#circuit-jfet-self-bias>).

### Find the bias point

For negligible gate current, **VGS ≈ −ID × RS**. This relation follows from Ohm's law across the source resistor.

The JFET has **IDSS = 6 mA** and **VGS(off) = −2 V** at 27 °C.

In the saturation region, its drain current follows:

**ID = IDSS × (1 − VGS / VGS(off))².**

The source resistor and the device equation must agree at the bias point. With 1 kilohm and a 12 V supply, current is approximately 1.131 mA.

The gate to source voltage is approximately −1.131 V. A larger source resistor produces a smaller current in this example.

### Supply voltage and operating region

Select the low supply view. At low supply voltage, the drain voltage does not support the saturation-region current.

The current then depends strongly on the supply voltage. This is different from the nearly flat portion of the curve at higher supply voltage.

JFET saturation does not have the same meaning as BJT saturation. The JFET saturation region is the useful region for this current-source example.

This model sets channel-length modulation to zero. Its flat current plateau excludes the finite output resistance of a physical JFET.

JFET parameters can vary between devices. Check the bias point across the specified parameter range.

## LTspice example: N-channel and P-channel switches

This example extends [power switching](#power-switching). Both loads use the same resistance, and both gates receive the same 0 to 5 V pulse.

[Open this circuit beside its topic](</docs/Discrete-Components/Semicondctors/MOSFETs#circuit-mosfet-switches>).

### Read voltage relative to the source

The N-channel source connects to ground. A 5 V gate voltage gives **VGS = +5 V** and turns this device on.

Its drain voltage falls toward ground. The load connects between the supply and the drain, so the load receives nearly the full supply voltage.

The P-channel source connects to the 5 V supply. A 0 V gate voltage gives **VGS = −5 V** and turns this device on.

Its drain voltage rises toward the supply. The load connects between the drain and ground, so the load receives nearly the full supply voltage.

A 5 V gate voltage gives the P-channel device **VGS = 0 V** and turns it off. Gate voltage alone does not identify the switch state.

### Compare load current

For an ideal switch, load current is **5 V / load resistance**. With a 100-ohm load, the ideal current is 50 mA.

The simulated on current is approximately 49.07 mA. Channel resistance and model series resistance account for the difference.

These circuits demonstrate polarity and load placement. They do not form a half bridge because their drains connect to separate loads.

### Model limits

The symmetric models use thresholds of +2 V and −2 V. They include finite junction capacitance to represent charge storage during switching.

Switching loss depends on gate drive, device capacitance, and load conditions. Inductive loads also require a path for stored energy.

Study [gate-drive dynamics](#8-gate-drive-dynamics) and the [body diode](#body-diode) before applying this arrangement to a physical load.

### MOSFET current mirror {#ltspice-mosfet-current-mirror}

Two MOSFETs share gate and source voltages to copy a reference current.

The reference transistor connects its drain to its gate. The reference current sets the common gate voltage.

The output transistor needs enough drain voltage to remain in saturation. Below that voltage, the current falls.

Channel-length modulation gives the output curve a slope. Equal gate voltage alone does not guarantee identical current at different drain voltages.

### JFET and BJT feedback amplifier {#ltspice-jfet-bjt-feedback}

A JFET input, PNP gain transistor, and NPN follower share a feedback path.

A rising gate voltage increases JFET current and lowers its drain voltage. The PNP transistor then raises the follower base voltage.

The follower output returns through a resistor to the JFET source. This opposes the initial gate to source voltage change.

The feedback resistor and source resistor largely set the gain. Changing this resistor also changes the DC operating point.

### JFET input stage with op-amp feedback {#ltspice-jfet-opamp-feedback}

An op-amp holds the JFET drain voltage near a reference while returning feedback to its source.

A fixed drain voltage and drain resistor establish nearly constant drain current.

The source must then follow input changes to keep the gate to source voltage nearly constant.

The output supplies the required source correction through the feedback resistor. The resulting gain is approximately one plus the resistor ratio.

A small capacitor adds a direct high-frequency feedback path around the op-amp.

### JFET differential pair inside a feedback loop {#ltspice-jfet-pair-feedback}

The first gate receives the input. A divided output voltage drives the second gate.

The op-amp senses the difference between the two drain voltages. Its output moves until the gate voltages nearly agree.

The resistor divider sets the closed-loop gain. The JFET pair provides high input resistance at the signal input.

The compensation capacitor connects the output to the drain at the op-amp negative input. This supplies a direct negative-feedback path at high frequency.
